Many freelancers in Switzerland never deal with VAT, and many others have to from their first invoice. Which group you belong to depends on your turnover, your clients and, sometimes, your own choice. This guide walks through the decision and shows with real numbers what registration changes.

## When you must register

A sole proprietor follows the same rule as any company: you are liable for VAT once your business turnover reaches **CHF 100,000 a year**. Three details catch freelancers out.

- **Turnover, not profit.** The threshold is about what you invoice (without VAT), not what you keep after costs.
- **Clients abroad count.** Fees from foreign clients are part of the CHF 100,000, even though many of them will not be charged Swiss VAT.
- **Exempt work does not count.** Turnover from activities that are exempt from VAT, such as therapy by licensed health professionals, teaching or insurance broking, is left out of the calculation.

**Starting out.** If you can reasonably expect to invoice more than CHF 100,000 within your first 12 months, you are liable from day one. Otherwise you check your turnover after each business year: once a year's turnover reaches the threshold, you must register. Either way, you have **30 days** to register with the ESTV once you are liable. If you register late, the ESTV can register you retroactively and you owe VAT on fees you already invoiced without it.

## When you may register voluntarily

Below the threshold you may give up the exemption and register voluntarily, from the start of the current tax period at the earliest. You must then stay registered for at least one full tax period (a calendar year).

| Pros                                                                        | Cons                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| You can deduct the VAT you pay on equipment, software, rent and other costs | Private clients pay 8.1 % more, or you earn less if you keep your prices  |
| Useful when you are investing (a car, machines, fitting out a workshop)     | Returns every quarter or half-year, with deadlines and records to keep    |
| Business clients do not mind: they deduct your VAT as their input tax       | You must keep it for at least one year and deregister actively afterwards |
| A VAT number signals an established business to some clients                | Mistakes on returns or invoices can cost money                            |

A rule of thumb: voluntary registration pays off when most of your clients are **VAT-registered businesses** (they recover the VAT, so it costs them nothing) and you have **significant costs or investments** with VAT on them. It rarely pays off if you mainly work for **private individuals**, because for them your price simply rises.

If your turnover later falls below CHF 100,000 and you do not expect to reach it the next year, you must deregister at the end of the period, or keep the registration by choice. Not deregistering counts as a voluntary registration.

## The net tax rate method for freelancers

Most freelancers who register choose the **net tax rate method** (Saldosteuersatz), because it removes most of the bookkeeping. You still show 8.1 % VAT on your invoices, but you pay the ESTV a fixed share of your gross fees, VAT included, and you no longer track input tax.

The ESTV sets one rate per industry. Some examples from the current list:

| Activity                                       | Net tax rate |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------ |
| Consulting (not listed elsewhere), IT services | 6.2 %        |
| Journalism, fiduciary office, graphic studio   | 6.2 %        |
| Architecture and engineering offices           | 6.2 %        |
| Photographers                                  | 5.3 %        |
| Translation office                             | 6.8 %        |

You must apply within **60 days** of receiving your VAT number; otherwise you have to stay with the effective method for at least three years. Returns are due **twice a year**, 60 days after 30 June and 31 December. The method is open to businesses with up to CHF 5,024,000 turnover and up to CHF 108,000 of tax a year, which covers virtually every freelancer. If you carry out a second activity that brings in more than 10 % of your turnover, it gets its own rate.

## What changes on your invoices

Once registered, every invoice to a client in Switzerland must show:

- your name and place as you appear in business, and your **VAT number** (CHE-123.456.789 MWST);
- the client's name and place;
- the date or period of the service, if it differs from the invoice date;
- what you supplied and how much;
- the price, and the **VAT rate and amount** (or just the rate if your prices include VAT).

Under the net tax rate method you still show the normal rate, 8.1 %, never your net tax rate.

Services to **clients abroad** are generally taxed where the client is established, so they are invoiced without Swiss VAT; the invoice should make clear who and where the client is. Before you register, the opposite rule applies: **never show VAT on an invoice**. Anyone who shows VAT on an invoice owes it to the ESTV, registered or not.

Check your contracts too. A price agreed "excluding VAT" lets you add 8.1 % after registering; a price agreed as a fixed amount usually means the VAT now comes out of your pocket.

## A worked example

Lena is a freelance IT consultant in Bern. She invoices **CHF 120,000** a year in fees to Swiss clients. Her business costs, such as software, hardware and coworking space, are **CHF 15,000** plus 8.1 % VAT (CHF 1,215).

Once registered, she adds 8.1 % to her fees:

|                       |   Amount (CHF) |
| --------------------- | -------------: |
| Fees                  |     120,000.00 |
| VAT charged at 8.1 %  |       9,720.00 |
| **Invoiced in total** | **129,720.00** |

**With the effective method** she pays the VAT charged minus the VAT she paid on costs:

|                         | Amount (CHF) |
| ----------------------- | -----------: |
| VAT charged             |     9,720.00 |
| Less input tax on costs |    −1,215.00 |
| **Owed to the ESTV**    | **8,505.00** |

**With the net tax rate method** she pays 6.2 % of her gross fees and ignores input tax:

|                          | Amount (CHF) |
| ------------------------ | -----------: |
| Gross fees, VAT included |   129,720.00 |
| × net tax rate 6.2 %     | **8,042.64** |

Here the net tax rate method saves her about CHF 460 a year, and she files two returns instead of four. The two methods would cost the same if she paid about CHF 1,677 of input tax, which means roughly CHF 20,700 of costs before VAT. With higher costs, or a large investment, the effective method would come out ahead.

Either way her fee income stays CHF 120,000, provided her clients accept the price plus VAT. Business clients will, because they deduct the CHF 9,720 as input tax. For a private client, the same work now costs CHF 129,720.
